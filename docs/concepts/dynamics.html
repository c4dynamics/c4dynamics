Dynamics#

The use cases of c4dynamics fly, drive, and steer different vehicles: a quadcopter, a missile, a car, and a helicopter. They look different, but the mechanics that move them are the same. Newton’s second law translates the forces on a body into its linear acceleration, and Euler’s equations translate the moments into its angular acceleration. These equations do not depend on the vehicle, and c4dynamics implements them once, for all vehicles (eqm6, eqm6q, rigidbody.inteqm).

What makes a quadcopter different from a missile is not the equations of motion, but the physics that generates the forces and the moments: rotors that push along one body axis, a rocket motor and a body that produces lift as it meets the air at an angle, tires that hold the car on its path, or a pivot that constrains the helicopter to two rotations. The control inputs act on the vehicle only through these forces and moments. Therefore, this page focuses on the force and moment model of each vehicle: what produces the forces and the moments, and how the control inputs shape them.

The workflow is shared by all the vehicles:

  1. Define a state object with the state variables of the vehicle.

  2. Compute the forces and the moments from the vehicle physics and the control inputs.

  3. Integrate the equations of motion over a time step and update the state.

  4. Store the state and analyze the results.

The state object is the common interface between the vehicle and the algorithm. The vehicle model changes, but the state object keeps the same interface: X to get and set the state vector, store and data to record and retrieve the history, and plot to view it. Therefore, a guidance law, a controller, or a filter that is developed on one system moves to another system smoothly, by replacing the state object and the force model, while the rest of the program remains intact.

The Common Core#

For a rigid body of mass \(m\) and inertia matrix \(I\), the Newton-Euler equations are:

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \dot{r} &= v \\ m \cdot \dot{v} &= F \\ I \cdot \dot{\omega} &= M - \omega \times I \cdot \omega \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

Where:

  • \(r, v\) are the position and the velocity in the inertial frame

  • \(F\) is the total force, resolved in the inertial frame

  • \(\omega = [p, q, r]\) is the angular rate of the body, resolved in the body frame

  • \(M\) is the total moment about the center of mass, resolved in the body frame

To close the loop, the attitude is propagated from the body rates \(\omega\). The attitude can be represented in different ways, and the representation sets the attitude part of the state vector:

  • Euler angles \([\varphi, \theta, \psi]\): intuitive, but singular at \(\theta = \pm 90°\) (rigidbody, 12 states).

  • A quaternion \([q_w, q_x, q_y, q_z]\): free of singularities at any attitude (quatbody, 13 states).

  • A rotation matrix: used to move vectors between the body and the inertial frames (BR, RB).

The kinematics of the Euler angles and of the quaternion are derived in Kinematics (see Quaternion Kinematics), and the conversions between the representations are given in Rigid Body Transformations. Below, the quadcopter section shows the quaternion form, and the missile section shows the Euler angles form.

The vehicle physics supplies \(F\) and \(M\). The following table summarizes the force and moment models used across the use cases. Each model is described in a dedicated section below.

Quadcopter#

Forces and moments

A quadcopter has no control surfaces. Every force and moment, other than gravity and drag, comes from four fixed rotors that all point along the body up axis. The control input is the vector of the four rotor speeds:

\[\mathbf{u} = [\Omega_1, \Omega_2, \Omega_3, \Omega_4]^T\]

A controller that commands the total thrust and the three torques, \([T, \tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z]\), maps them to the rotor speeds by the inverse of the thrust-torque mixing matrix below.

The equations of this section are written in the 'ENU' convention:

  • Body frame: \(x\) forward (between rotors 1 and 3), \(y\) left, \(z\) up.

  • Inertial frame: \(x\) east, \(y\) north, \(z\) up.

  • Rotation: 3-2-1 (\(ZYX\)), yaw \(\rightarrow\) pitch \(\rightarrow\) roll.

In the 'NED' convention (body frame forward-right-down), the up direction flips the sign of the thrust, the gravity, and the moment terms, as detailed in Quadcopter Equations of Motion.

Rotors

The rotors are arranged in an \(X\) configuration, at a distance \(L\) from the center of mass:

Rotor

Position

Direction

\(\Omega_1\)

front-right

CCW

\(\Omega_2\)

rear-left

CCW

\(\Omega_3\)

front-left

CW

\(\Omega_4\)

rear-right

CW

Each rotor produces a thrust and a reaction torque, both proportional to the square of its speed:

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} F_i &= k_T \cdot \Omega_i^2 \\ Q_i &= k_Q \cdot \Omega_i^2 \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

The four speeds map into one force and three control moments:

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} T &= F_1 + F_2 + F_3 + F_4 \\ \tau_x &= L \cdot (-F_1 + F_2 + F_3 - F_4) \\ \tau_y &= L \cdot (-F_1 + F_2 - F_3 + F_4) \\ \tau_z &= {k_Q \over k_T} \cdot (-F_1 - F_2 + F_3 + F_4) \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

Where:

  • \(k_T\) is the thrust coefficient \([N / (rad/s)^2]\)

  • \(k_Q\) is the torque coefficient \([N \cdot m / (rad/s)^2]\)

  • \(L\) is the distance of the rotors from the center of mass \([m]\)

The signs of the torque mapping follow from the positive direction of the body rates. Positive roll is a rotation about \(x\) that moves rotors 1 and 4 down, so it is driven by extra thrust on rotors 2 and 3. Positive pitch is a rotation about \(y\) that moves rotors 1 and 3 down, so it is driven by extra thrust on rotors 2 and 4. Positive yaw is a counterclockwise rotation about \(z\) when looking from above. Each rotor’s reaction torque turns the body against the rotor’s spin, so positive yaw is driven by the clockwise rotors 3 and 4.

Each of the four outputs has a physical meaning:

  • Collective thrust \(T\) acts along the body up axis. It is the only control force. To accelerate sideways, the vehicle must first tilt.

  • Roll and pitch moments \(\tau_x, \tau_y\): a thrust difference between the left and the right rotors, or between the front and the rear rotors, acting on the arm \(L\).

  • Yaw moment \(\tau_z\): the reaction torques \(Q_i\). A rotor turning counterclockwise pushes the body clockwise. When the counterclockwise pair spins faster than the clockwise pair, the torques do not cancel, and the vehicle yaws, with little change in the total thrust.

Forces

The body force is the total thrust along the body up axis and a linear aerodynamic drag that opposes the body velocity:

\[\begin{split}\mathbf{F_b} = \begin{bmatrix} -A_x \cdot u \\ -A_y \cdot v \\ T - A_z \cdot w \end{bmatrix}\end{split}\]

Where \(A_x, A_y, A_z\) are the aerodynamic drag coefficients along the body \(x, y, z\) axes, and \(\mathbf{v_b} = [u, v, w]^T\) is the velocity in the body frame. The body velocity is obtained by rotating the inertial velocity \(\mathbf{v_i} = [v_x, v_y, v_z]^T\) to the body frame:

\[\mathbf{v_b} = [BI] \cdot \mathbf{v_i}\]

Where \([BI]\) is the body from inertial DCM (direction cosine matrix), generated by the Euler angles in a 3-2-1 order. With a wind, the drag acts on the air-relative velocity, \(\mathbf{v_i} - \mathbf{v_{wind}}\).

The body force is rotated to the inertial frame and combined with gravity \(g\) in the negative \(z\) direction:

\[\begin{split}\mathbf{F_i} = [BI]^T \cdot \mathbf{F_b} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ -m \cdot g \end{bmatrix}\end{split}\]

Where \([BI]^T\), the transpose of \([BI]\), rotates vectors to the inertial frame from the body frame, and \(m\) is the mass of the quadcopter \([kg]\). \(\mathbf{F_i}\) is the force \(F\) of the translational equations of the common core. \(\mathbf{F_b} / m\) is the specific force, i.e. what an accelerometer at the center of mass measures.

Moments

The net moments consist of the control torques, an aerodynamic damping, and a gyroscopic coupling:

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} M_x &= \tau_x - A_r \cdot p - I_R \cdot q \cdot \Omega \\ M_y &= \tau_y - A_r \cdot q + I_R \cdot p \cdot \Omega \\ M_z &= \tau_z - A_r \cdot r \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

Where the net rotor angular speed is:

\[\Omega = \Omega_1 + \Omega_2 - \Omega_3 - \Omega_4\]

And:

  • \(\tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z\) are the roll, pitch, and yaw control torques generated by the rotors.

  • \(A_r\) is the aerodynamic rotational drag coefficient. The terms \(A_r \cdot p, A_r \cdot q, A_r \cdot r\) are damping moments that oppose the corresponding angular rates.

  • \(I_R\) is the rotor moment of inertia. The terms \(I_R \cdot q \cdot \Omega\) and \(I_R \cdot p \cdot \Omega\) are gyroscopic moments produced by the angular momentum of the spinning rotors. They couple the roll and the pitch dynamics: a pitch rate generates a roll moment, and a roll rate generates a pitch moment.

\([M_x, M_y, M_z]\) is the moment \(M\) of the rotational equations of the common core.

Implementation

quadforces returns the body force (thrust and drag) and the body moment. quadeqm rotates the force to the inertial frame, adds gravity, and evaluates the Newton-Euler equations, for the 'NED' and 'ENU' frame conventions, with the signature of scipy.integrate.solve_ivp. The physical parameters of the reference vehicle are given by c4dynamics.models.quad.default_quad_config().

Quaternion attitude

A quadcopter can flip, roll, and point its up axis in any direction. The Euler angles become singular at \(\theta = \pm 90°\), so the quadcopter is a natural candidate for the quaternion representation. A quatbody object holds 13 state variables:

\[X = [x, y, z, v_x, v_y, v_z, q_w, q_x, q_y, q_z, p, q, r]^T\]

Where:

  • \(x, y, z\) are the position coordinates in the inertial frame

  • \(v_x, v_y, v_z\) are the velocity components in the inertial frame

  • \(q_w, q_x, q_y, q_z\) are the attitude quaternion components (scalar first)

  • \(p, q, r\) are the body roll, pitch, and yaw rates

The translational and the angular rates equations are those of the common core. The quaternion \(\mathbf{q} = [q_w, q_x, q_y, q_z]\) is propagated by the body rates:

\[\dot{\mathbf{q}} = {1 \over 2} \cdot \mathbf{q} \otimes [0, p, q, r]\]

These rates contain no trigonometric functions and no division, and are regular at any attitude. Numerical integration does not preserve the unit norm exactly, so the quaternion is normalized after each step. The rotation matrices and the Euler angles (phi, theta, psi) are computed from the quaternion, for the force model and for presentation.

The attitude representation is independent of the force model. quadeqm accepts a 12-state rigidbody as well, with the Euler angles kinematics shown in the missile section below. See Quaternion Kinematics for the derivation and Rigid Body Transformations for the conversions between the representations.

Example

Define the quadcopter, speed up the counterclockwise rotors by 10%, slow down the clockwise rotors by 10%, and integrate the equations of motion:

>>> import numpy as np
>>> import c4dynamics as c4d
>>> from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
>>> from c4dynamics.models.quad import default_quad_config

>>> quad = c4d.quatbody()
>>> for k, v in default_quad_config().items():
...   setattr(quad, k, v)
>>> w_hover = np.sqrt(quad.m * quad.g / 4 / quad.kT)          # rotor speed that carries the weight
>>> rotor_speeds = w_hover * np.array([1.1, 1.1, 0.9, 0.9])   # ccw pair faster, cw pair slower

>>> F_b, M_b = c4d.eqm.quadforces(quad.X, quad, rotor_speeds, 'ENU')
>>> F_b      # thrust along body up
array([0, 0, 4.63...])
>>> M_b      # a yaw moment only
array([0, 0, -0.035...])

>>> dt = 0.01
>>> for t in np.arange(0, 2, dt):
...   quad.store(t)
...   sol = solve_ivp(c4d.eqm.quadeqm, [t, t + dt], quad.X, args = (quad, rotor_speeds, 'ENU'))
...   quad.X = sol.y[:, -1]
...   quad.quat = quad.quat    # normalize to a unit quaternion

>>> print(quad)
[ x  y  z  vx  vy  vz  qw  qx  qy  qz  p  q  r ]
>>> quad.quat
array([ 0.985...,  0.        ,  0.        , -0.170...])
>>> quad.psi * c4d.r2d     # turns clockwise seen from above
-19.6...

The reaction torques turn the vehicle about its up axis. The quaternion is a rotation of \(\alpha = -19.6°\) about \(z\): \(q_w = cos(\alpha / 2)\), \(q_z = sin(\alpha / 2)\). The total thrust is slightly above the weight, and the vehicle climbs slowly. Replacing c4d.quatbody() by c4d.rigidbody() selects the Euler angles model with no other change in the code.

Use cases

  • Cascade PID Control: a cascade PID controller flies the quadcopter on a figure-eight trajectory.

  • EKF Estimation: an extended Kalman filter estimates the quadcopter state in closed loop with the controller.

  • ArduPilot Simulation: the ArduPilot flight controller flies a quatbody quadcopter in a figure-eight.

Missile#

State vector

The missile is a rigid body with six degrees of freedom, represented by a rigidbody object:

\[X = [x, y, z, v_x, v_y, v_z, \varphi, \theta, \psi, p, q, r]^T\]

The variables are as defined for the quadcopter. The mass and the moments of inertia are attributes of the object, mass and I, and may be updated during the run, as the rocket motor burns its fuel.

The target is a point mass with three degrees of freedom, represented by a datapoint object:

\[X = [x, y, z, v_x, v_y, v_z]^T\]

Dynamics

The forces on the missile are the propulsion along the body \(x\) axis, the aerodynamic lift and drag, and gravity. The propulsion and the aerodynamic forces are computed in the body frame and rotated to the inertial frame by RB. The aerodynamic moments are computed in the body frame from the angles of attack and the fins deflections.

The translational equations are Newton’s second law in the inertial frame:

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \dot{r} &= v \\ \dot{v} &= {F \over m} \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

The rotational equations relate the moments to the body rates, and the body rates to the Euler angles rates:

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \dot{\varphi} &= p + (q \cdot sin(\varphi) + r \cdot cos(\varphi)) \cdot tan(\theta) \\ \dot{\theta} &= q \cdot cos(\varphi) - r \cdot sin(\varphi) \\ \dot{\psi} &= {q \cdot sin(\varphi) + r \cdot cos(\varphi) \over cos(\theta)} \\ \dot{p} &= {M[0] - q \cdot r \cdot (I_{zz} - I_{yy}) \over I_{xx}} \\ \dot{q} &= {M[1] - p \cdot r \cdot (I_{xx} - I_{zz}) \over I_{yy}} \\ \dot{r} &= {M[2] - p \cdot q \cdot (I_{yy} - I_{xx}) \over I_{zz}} \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

The derivatives are given by eqm6. rigidbody.inteqm integrates them by a fourth-order Runge-Kutta step, and datapoint.inteqm integrates the translational equations of the target (eqm3). The full derivation is given in Kinematics.

Example

Launch the missile at \(30°\) pitch in a NED frame (\(z\) down) with a constant thrust, and propagate a non-maneuvering target:

>>> missile = c4d.rigidbody(vx = 30 * np.cos(30 * c4d.d2r), vz = -30 * np.sin(30 * c4d.d2r), theta = 30 * c4d.d2r)
>>> missile.mass = 85               # kg
>>> missile.I = [0.5, 61, 61]       # kg.m^2
>>> target = c4d.datapoint(x = 4000, z = -1000, vx = -250)

>>> dt = 0.01
>>> for t in np.arange(0, 3, dt):
...   missile.store(t)
...   target.store(t)
...   thrust  = missile.RB @ [3000, 0, 0]               # body x to inertial
...   gravity = [0, 0, missile.mass * c4d.g_ms2]        # z down
...   missile.inteqm(thrust + gravity, [0, 0, 0], dt)   # forces, moments, dt
...   target.inteqm([0, 0, 0], dt)

>>> missile.vel_mag()
123.8...
>>> target.x
3250.0...

In the use case, the moments of the aerodynamics and the control fins replace the zero moments vector, and the guidance law closes the loop.

Use cases

  • Proportional Navigation Guidance: a 6DOF missile, guided by proportional navigation, intercepts a target.

Car#

State vector

The car is modeled in the plane, by its position and its heading. It is represented by a state object with the state variables:

\[X = [x, y, \psi]^T\]

Where:

  • \(x, y\) are the position coordinates in the plane

  • \(\psi\) is the heading angle with respect to the \(x\) axis

Dynamics

At a constant speed \(V\), the kinematic (unicycle) model of the car is given by:

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \dot{x} &= V \cdot cos(\psi) \\ \dot{y} &= V \cdot sin(\psi) \\ \dot{\psi} &= \delta \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

Where \(\delta\) is the steering input, the rate of change of the heading.

For a straight reference line along the \(x\) axis, the model is linearized about \(\psi = 0\). The longitudinal motion decouples, and the lateral dynamics reduce to two state variables, \(X = [\psi, y]^T\), with \(\dot{\psi} = \delta\) and \(\dot{y} = V \cdot \psi\). This is the prediction model of the MPC use case.

Example

Define the car with an initial lateral offset of \(1m\), steer it back to the line with a simple feedback, and store the steering input as a parameter of the state:

>>> V = 22.3   # m/s
>>> def car_model(t, X, delta):
...   x, y, psi = X
...   return [V * np.cos(psi), V * np.sin(psi), delta]

>>> car = c4d.state(x = 0, y = 1, psi = 0)
>>> car.delta = 0

>>> dt = 0.2
>>> for t in np.arange(0, 8, dt):
...   car.store(t)
...   car.storeparams('delta', t = t)
...   car.delta = -0.5 * car.y - 2 * car.psi
...   sol = solve_ivp(car_model, [t, t + dt], car.X, args = (car.delta,))
...   car.X = sol.y[:, -1]

>>> print(car)
[ x  y  ψ ]
>>> car.y      # back on the line
-0.02...

The same car seen by a camera is a point in the image plane. The car tracker use cases represent it by a pixelpoint, \(X = [x, y, w, h]^T\) (the center pixel and the bounding box size), and propagate it with a constant velocity model in a kalman filter.

Use cases

  • MPC Steering: a model predictive controller steers the car to a straight reference line.

  • Car Tracker (YOLOv3) and Car Tracker (YOLO11): a Kalman filter tracks a car detected in a video, with constant velocity dynamics.

Helicopter#

State vector

The helicopter is a laboratory-scale model with two degrees of freedom. It is mounted on a fixed base, and rotates in pitch and yaw only, with no translational motion. It is represented by the helicopter environment, a subclass of state (c4dynamics.envs.environments.helicopter), with the state variables:

\[X = [\theta, \psi, \dot{\theta}, \dot{\psi}]^T\]

Where:

  • \(\theta, \psi\) are the pitch (elevation) and yaw (azimuth) angles

  • \(\dot{\theta}, \dot{\psi}\) are the pitch and yaw angular rates

Dynamics

Two rotors, driven by the voltages \(V_p\) and \(V_y\), generate the pitch and the yaw torques. The equations of motion, derived by Lagrangian mechanics, are:

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} (J_p + M \cdot L_{cm}^2) \cdot \ddot{\theta} &= K_{pp} \cdot V_p + K_{py} \cdot V_y \\ &\quad - M \cdot g \cdot L_{cm} \cdot cos(\theta) - D_p \cdot \dot{\theta} \\ &\quad - M \cdot L_{cm}^2 \cdot \dot{\psi}^2 \cdot sin(\theta) \cdot cos(\theta) \\ (J_y + M \cdot L_{cm}^2 \cdot cos^2(\theta)) \cdot \ddot{\psi} &= K_{yp} \cdot V_p + K_{yy} \cdot V_y \\ &\quad - D_y \cdot \dot{\psi} \\ &\quad + 2 \cdot M \cdot L_{cm}^2 \cdot \dot{\psi} \cdot \dot{\theta} \cdot sin(\theta) \cdot cos(\theta) \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

Where:

  • \(J_p, J_y\) are the pitch and yaw moments of inertia

  • \(M, L_{cm}\) are the mass and the center of mass distance from the pivot

  • \(D_p, D_y\) are the pitch and yaw viscous damping

  • \(K_{pp}, K_{py}, K_{yp}, K_{yy}\) are the torque gains from each rotor to each axis

  • \(g\) is the gravity acceleration

The yaw-pitch coupling terms (centrifugal and Coriolis) and the cross rotor gains make the system a nonlinear multi-input multi-output plant. The equations are written in the control-affine form:

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \dot{X}_1 &= X_2 \\ \dot{X}_2 &= F(X) + G(X) \cdot u \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

with \(X_1 = [\theta, \psi]^T\), \(X_2 = [\dot{\theta}, \dot{\psi}]^T\), and \(u = [V_p, V_y]^T\). The helicopter object provides F(X), G(X), and dynamics(t, X, u), the full state derivative with the signature of scipy.integrate.solve_ivp.

Example

Find the trim voltages, \(G \cdot u = -F\), that hold the helicopter at rest, add a step to the pitch voltage, and integrate the equations of motion:

>>> from c4dynamics.envs.environments import helicopter
>>> heli = helicopter(theta = 0, psi = 0)
>>> u_trim = np.linalg.solve(heli.G(), -heli.F())
>>> u = u_trim + [2, 0]   # V

>>> dt = 0.01
>>> for t in np.arange(0, 2, dt):
...   heli.store(t)
...   sol = solve_ivp(heli.dynamics, [t, t + dt], heli.X, args = (u,))
...   heli.X = sol.y[:, -1]

>>> print(heli)
[ θ  ψ  dtheta  dpsi ]
>>> heli.theta * c4d.r2d, heli.psi * c4d.r2d   # pitch up, and a coupled yaw
(10.4..., -2.5...)
